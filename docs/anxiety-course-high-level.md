# Anxiety Course: High-Level Architecture

Status: Draft for high-level approval  
Working title: **Anxiety: From Alarm to Action**

This document defines the course promise, scope, progression, sections, units,
and lesson nodes. It intentionally stops before mid-level lesson specifications
and low-level exercise payloads.

## 1. Course contract

### Learner

Adults with no prior CBT knowledge who experience worry, nervous anticipation,
avoidance, reassurance seeking, panic-like sensations, or social and performance
anxiety. Language must remain clear for secondary-English readers.

### Course outcome

By the end, a learner can:

1. map an anxiety cycle across sensations, thoughts, attention, and actions;
2. distinguish a solvable problem from hypothetical worry;
3. notice avoidance and safety behaviours that create short relief;
4. choose a response that fits the process maintaining anxiety;
5. plan, complete, and review safe gradual-approach practice;
6. build a flexible plan for setbacks and professional support.

Completion proves skill use in changed situations. It does not require anxiety
to fall, a perfect streak, or personal disclosure.

### Delivery profile

- 6 sections
- 21 units
- 169 course nodes
- Minimum 3 units per section
- Minimum 7 nodes per unit
- Unit sizes vary from 7 to 10 nodes according to curriculum depth
- 5 to 9 minutes per node
- Flexible pace with no clinical completion deadline
- At 5 to 7 nodes per week, the complete path lasts roughly 24 to 34 weeks
- Reflection remains optional and unscored
- Pausing or skipping never removes progress

### Why the counts vary

| Section | Units | Nodes | Why this depth is needed |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| 1. Understand the Alarm | 3 | 22 | Foundation, cycle mapping, and safety boundaries |
| 2. Notice Without Fighting | 3 | 24 | Attention, grounding, acceptance, and choice need separate practice |
| 3. Work With Worry and Uncertainty | 4 | 32 | Worry, problem-solving, certainty seeking, and experiments are distinct skills |
| 4. Break the Avoidance Loop | 4 | 36 | Gradual approach requires preparation, practice, repetition, and transfer |
| 5. Apply Across Anxiety Patterns | 4 | 32 | Pattern recognition and tool selection require separate units |
| 6. Build a Life Larger Than Anxiety | 3 | 23 | Values, setbacks, maintenance, and care planning close the path |
| **Total** | **21** | **169** | Counts follow learning requirements, not a fixed unit template |

### Scope boundary

This is self-guided psychoeducation and skill practice. It does not diagnose an
anxiety disorder or replace treatment. It does not provide trauma processing,
OCD exposure and response prevention, medication advice, or unsupervised intense
interoceptive exposure. New or concerning physical symptoms, severe functional
impairment, self-neglect, self-harm risk, and persistent worsening require an
appropriate clinical or urgent-care route.

## 2. Evidence-based direction

The main path is transdiagnostic CBT because shared CBT skills can help across
anxiety presentations. A transdiagnostic protocol performed comparably to
diagnosis-specific protocols in a
[randomized trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28768327/). This does not erase
the need for disorder-specific assessment and care.

CBT is the backbone. Grounding, breathing, relaxation, and mindfulness are
support skills. WHO strongly recommends
[brief structured CBT-based interventions](https://www.who.int/teams/mental-health-and-substance-use/treatment-care/mental-health-gap-action-programme/evidence-centre/anxiety/brief-structured-psychological-interventions)
for adults with GAD or panic. Its recommendation for relaxation or mindfulness
stress management is conditional and based on low-certainty evidence.

NICE recommends
[CBT-based self-help materials worked through systematically](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg113/chapter/Recommendations)
for GAD. NICE places marked impairment and high-risk presentations outside
simple self-help.

### Decisions this architecture rejects

- No fixed Day 1, Day 3, Day 7, or Day 30 readiness stages.
- No requirement to calm the body before learning or acting.
- No unlock based on symptom-rating reduction.
- No assumption that breathing or grounding fits every anxiety pattern.
- No use of streaks, completion count, or reflection as mastery evidence.
- No identical unit or lesson count applied across the course.

## 3. Learning spine

```text
Recognize the alarm
  -> Map the maintaining cycle
  -> Observe without immediate escape
  -> Work with worry and uncertainty
  -> Find avoidance and safety behaviours
  -> Approach gradually and learn
  -> Transfer skills across anxiety patterns
  -> Act by values and maintain the learning
```

This spine defines the course sequence. Individual lessons do not all follow the
same interaction pattern. Each lesson must use the cognitive action required by
its objective.

## 4. Course path

### Section 1: Understand the Alarm

**Section capability:** Explain what anxiety is, map its maintaining cycle, and
recognize when self-guided anxiety skills are or are not appropriate.

#### Unit 1.1: The Alarm System · 7 nodes

1. **Anxiety Has a Job** — Distinguish a protective alarm from proof of danger.
2. **How the Body Prepares** — Recognize common alarm sensations and their function.
3. **How the Mind Predicts** — Identify threat predictions without treating them as facts.
4. **How Urges Protect** — Connect fight, flight, freeze, and appease urges to protection.
5. **Fear and Anxiety** — Distinguish a present threat from anticipation of a possible future threat.
6. **Intensity Is Not Evidence** — Separate how strong a feeling is from how likely a threat is.
7. **Alarm-System Checkpoint** — Explain the alarm model in a changed scenario.

#### Unit 1.2: The Anxiety Cycle · 8 nodes

1. **The Trigger** — Identify external, internal, and uncertain starting cues.
2. **The Meaning Added** — Find the interpretation that turns a cue into a threat.
3. **The Body Feedback Loop** — Explain how sensations can amplify threat meaning.
4. **Attention Narrows** — Notice how the mind searches for confirming evidence.
5. **The Action Urge** — Connect threat meaning with escape, checking, or control.
6. **Short Relief** — Identify the immediate reward produced by protective action.
7. **What the Brain Learns** — Explain how short relief can strengthen future alarm.
8. **Cycle-Mapping Checkpoint** — Reconstruct the complete loop in a new context.

#### Unit 1.3: Anxiety Literacy and Boundaries · 7 nodes

1. **Normal Anxiety and Persistent Difficulty** — Compare occasional anxiety with patterns that impair life.
2. **Real Danger Comes First** — Choose practical safety action when a threat is real.
3. **Physical Symptoms Need Careful Thinking** — Avoid assuming that new symptoms are only anxiety.
4. **Function Matters** — Notice effects on sleep, work, study, relationships, and self-care.
5. **Different Anxiety Presentations** — Recognize broad patterns without self-diagnosing.
6. **What Self-Help Can and Cannot Do** — Set a truthful course expectation.
7. **Section Checkpoint: Explain the Pattern** — Integrate alarm, cycle, and care boundaries.

### Section 2: Notice Without Fighting

**Section capability:** Notice anxious attention, return to the present task, and
make space for discomfort before choosing an action.

#### Unit 2.1: Attention Under Threat · 8 nodes

1. **Threat Scanning** — Recognize the mind's search for possible danger.
2. **Tunnel Vision** — Notice what anxiety leaves outside attention.
3. **Attention Turned Inward** — Recognize monitoring of thoughts, sensations, or appearance.
4. **Selective Evidence** — Find how attention collects threat-confirming details.
5. **Attention Can Move** — Treat attention as a trainable action, not a fixed state.
6. **External Focus** — Practise noticing useful information outside the self.
7. **Return to the Task** — Place attention on what the situation actually requires.
8. **Attention Checkpoint** — Choose and explain an attention shift in varied scenarios.

#### Unit 2.2: Grounding and Present Focus · 7 nodes

1. **What Grounding Is For** — Frame grounding as re-engagement, not emotional erasure.
2. **Orient to Place and Time** — Use current context to separate now from prediction.
3. **Use the Senses Deliberately** — Select sensory information that supports the next action.
4. **Notice Physical Contact** — Use stable contact points without judging sensations.
5. **Breathing as Support** — Use breath gently without making perfect calm a requirement.
6. **Ground and Rejoin** — Return from the grounding skill to a chosen task.
7. **Grounding Checkpoint** — Distinguish helpful grounding from avoidance or reassurance.

#### Unit 2.3: Make Space, Then Choose · 9 nodes

1. **Name What Is Here** — Distinguish thought, sensation, emotion, image, and urge.
2. **A Thought Is an Event** — Step back from a prediction without debating it immediately.
3. **An Urge Is Not a Command** — Notice protective impulses while preserving choice.
4. **Allow a Manageable Feeling** — Practise willingness without forcing intensity.
5. **Making Room Is Not Liking It** — Separate acceptance from approval or resignation.
6. **Choose the Next Useful Action** — Act from the situation's needs rather than the urge.
7. **When Pausing Becomes Avoidance** — Recognize delay disguised as preparation.
8. **Combine Attention and Choice** — Use present focus to support deliberate action.
9. **Section Checkpoint: Pause and Choose** — Apply the complete sequence to a changed case.

### Section 3: Work With Worry and Uncertainty

**Section capability:** Distinguish productive planning from repetitive worry,
solve actionable problems, reduce certainty-seeking, and learn through small
uncertainty experiments.

#### Unit 3.1: What Worry Is Trying to Do · 8 nodes

1. **Worry Tries to Protect** — Identify the protective intention behind repeated thinking.
2. **Worry and Planning Are Different** — Compare circular thought with an actionable plan.
3. **If I Worry, I Am Prepared** — Examine the belief that worry prevents surprise.
4. **If I Stop, I Do Not Care** — Separate caring from repetitive mental effort.
5. **Mental Rehearsal Without Action** — Recognize repeated simulation that changes nothing.
6. **Questions With No Finish Line** — Identify worry questions that cannot produce certainty.
7. **The Worry-Relief Loop** — Map reassurance, checking, and temporary relief.
8. **Worry-Function Checkpoint** — Explain what worry promises and what it costs.

#### Unit 3.2: Problems and Hypothetical Worry · 9 nodes

1. **Is the Problem Happening Now?** — Locate the issue in the present or imagined future.
2. **Is Action Available?** — Decide whether a useful action can be taken now.
3. **What Is Controllable?** — Separate influence from outcomes outside control.
4. **Define One Problem Clearly** — Replace a broad fear with a specific solvable issue.
5. **Generate Possible Actions** — Produce realistic options without seeking a perfect one.
6. **Choose the Next Step** — Select a proportionate action using available information.
7. **Act and Review** — Learn from the result rather than restarting the entire worry loop.
8. **When No Action Is Available** — Shift from solving to tolerating uncertainty.
9. **Problem-or-Worry Checkpoint** — Route varied scenarios to the correct response.

#### Unit 3.3: Certainty Seeking · 7 nodes

1. **The Demand to Know** — Identify hidden rules that require guaranteed outcomes.
2. **Checking for Certainty** — Recognize repeated checks that never feel complete.
3. **Reassurance That Fades** — Explain why another answer brings only short relief.
4. **Research Without an End** — Distinguish useful information gathering from over-research.
5. **Decision Paralysis** — Connect certainty demands with delay and avoidance.
6. **Enough Information** — Choose a reasonable decision threshold.
7. **Certainty-Seeking Checkpoint** — Find and interrupt the maintaining loop.

#### Unit 3.4: Uncertainty Experiments · 8 nodes

1. **What an Experiment Tests** — Use action to learn rather than obtain reassurance.
2. **Choose a Safe Uncertainty** — Select a small everyday situation suitable for self-guided practice.
3. **State the Prediction** — Define the feared outcome in observable terms.
4. **Reduce One Protection** — Choose one nonessential safety behaviour to loosen.
5. **Take the Action** — Enter the uncertainty without requiring confidence first.
6. **Observe What Happened** — Record facts, coping, and incomplete information fairly.
7. **Update the Learning** — Revise the prediction without demanding certainty.
8. **Section Checkpoint: Plan the Next Test** — Generalize learning to a changed uncertainty.

### Section 4: Break the Avoidance Loop

**Section capability:** Identify avoidance and safety behaviours, design gradual
approach practice, learn from outcomes, and repeat across time and context.

#### Unit 4.1: Avoidance and Safety Behaviours · 9 nodes

1. **Avoidance Has Many Forms** — Recognize physical, mental, emotional, and digital avoidance.
2. **Escape After Starting** — Notice leaving, rushing, or ending contact early.
3. **Delay and Procrastination** — Identify postponement used to avoid discomfort.
4. **Subtle Safety Behaviours** — Find checking, rehearsing, distraction, and exit planning.
5. **Preparation or Protection?** — Distinguish useful preparation from threat-confirming rituals.
6. **The Life Cost** — Identify what avoidance removes from daily life.
7. **Why Relief Reinforces It** — Connect immediate relief with future repetition.
8. **Function Matters More Than Form** — Judge behaviour by what it is doing in context.
9. **Avoidance Checkpoint** — Classify varied actions without calling all protection unhealthy.

#### Unit 4.2: Build a Gradual Approach · 10 nodes

1. **Choose What Matters** — Link approach practice to a meaningful direction.
2. **Select One Target** — Define a specific safe situation affected by anxiety.
3. **Check the Safety Boundary** — Exclude real danger, medical risk, and specialist-only exposure.
4. **Break the Target Into Steps** — Create a progression without large jumps.
5. **Difficulty Is Personal** — Avoid assuming one universal order of steps.
6. **Choose a Repeatable Start** — Begin with a step that can produce useful learning.
7. **Name the Feared Prediction** — State what the learner expects will happen.
8. **Choose One Safety Behaviour to Reduce** — Keep the task focused and manageable.
9. **Plan Support Without Creating Dependence** — Use help that enables approach.
10. **Approach-Plan Checkpoint** — Review the plan for safety, meaning, and learnability.

#### Unit 4.3: Approach and Learn · 8 nodes

1. **Approach Is a Learning Task** — Define the purpose beyond enduring discomfort.
2. **Calm Is Not the Goal** — Measure engagement rather than symptom reduction.
3. **Stay With the Chosen Action** — Keep attention on the task without a fixed endurance rule.
4. **Notice the Pull to Escape** — Observe action urges without automatically following them.
5. **Compare Prediction and Outcome** — Look for visible evidence and coping.
6. **When Something Difficult Happens** — Learn coping without pretending feared outcomes are impossible.
7. **Avoid One-Event Conclusions** — Keep learning specific and provisional.
8. **Approach Checkpoint** — Interpret a changed practice outcome accurately.

#### Unit 4.4: Repeat and Generalize · 9 nodes

1. **Why One Attempt Is Not Enough** — Distinguish first evidence from stable learning.
2. **Repeat Across Time** — Retrieve the learning after a gap.
3. **Vary the Context** — Practise when surface details change.
4. **When Fear Returns** — Treat renewed anxiety as a cue to practise, not proof of failure.
5. **When a Step Feels Stuck** — Identify whether the prediction or safety behaviour changed.
6. **When the Step Is Too Large** — Reduce difficulty without abandoning direction.
7. **When the Step Is Too Easy** — Widen context or reduce unnecessary support.
8. **Keep the Useful Evidence** — Record learning without turning the record into reassurance.
9. **Section Checkpoint: Independent Approach Plan** — Build the next cycle of practice.

### Section 5: Apply Skills Across Anxiety Patterns

**Section capability:** Recognize common anxiety-maintaining patterns, match the
response to the process, and identify when tailored professional care is needed.

#### Unit 5.1: Panic and Feared Body Sensations · 8 nodes

1. **Panic Attack and Panic Disorder Are Different** — Learn the distinction without self-diagnosis.
2. **The Body-Sensation Loop** — Map sensation, catastrophic meaning, and amplified alarm.
3. **When Sensation Becomes the Threat** — Recognize fear of the internal experience itself.
4. **Body Monitoring** — Notice scanning that increases salience and uncertainty.
5. **Escape and Place Avoidance** — Connect fear of future attacks with restriction.
6. **What General Education Can Teach** — Use the shared anxiety model without prescribing treatment.
7. **Medical and Clinical Boundaries** — Route new symptoms and intense exposure appropriately.
8. **Panic-Pattern Checkpoint** — Distinguish shared skills from panic-specific care.

#### Unit 5.2: Social and Performance Anxiety · 9 nodes

1. **The Social-Threat Prediction** — Identify feared judgment, rejection, or visible anxiety.
2. **Attention Turned Inward** — Recognize monitoring of appearance and performance.
3. **Social Safety Behaviours** — Find rehearsing, hiding, overexplaining, and avoiding eye contact.
4. **Anxiety Before the Event** — Map anticipatory worry and preparation loops.
5. **Mind Reading** — Separate another person's observable behaviour from assumed judgment.
6. **The Replay Afterwards** — Recognize selective post-event review.
7. **Return Attention Outward** — Focus on the conversation or task instead of self-monitoring.
8. **Behavioural Learning Boundary** — Separate general experiments from disorder-specific CBT.
9. **Social-Pattern Checkpoint** — Match maintaining processes to appropriate responses.

#### Unit 5.3: Perfectionism, Checking, and Reassurance · 8 nodes

1. **The Perfect-Performance Rule** — Identify standards used to prevent all criticism or error.
2. **All-or-Nothing Standards** — Recognize rules that treat a small mistake as complete failure.
3. **Over-Preparation** — Notice excessive preparation used to prevent all uncertainty.
4. **Procrastination** — Connect delay with fear of imperfect performance.
5. **Checking That Never Finishes** — Map repeated checking and fading relief.
6. **Reassurance That Must Be Repeated** — Identify dependence on another person's certainty.
7. **Health and Reassurance Boundaries** — Avoid replacing medical care or reinforcing repeated reassurance.
8. **Protection-Pattern Checkpoint** — Compare perfectionism, checking, and reassurance loops.

#### Unit 5.4: Match the Tool and Care Path · 7 nodes

1. **Find the Maintaining Process** — Identify what is keeping the current anxiety loop active.
2. **When Problem-Solving Fits** — Choose action for a current, solvable problem.
3. **When Present Focus Fits** — Choose grounding or attention skills to rejoin the task.
4. **When Flexible Thinking Fits** — Examine a prediction without forcing positivity.
5. **When Uncertainty Practice Fits** — Loosen checking or reassurance in a safe situation.
6. **When Gradual Approach Fits** — Choose behavioural learning for safe avoided situations.
7. **Section Checkpoint: General Skill or Tailored Care?** — Match the tool and professional-care boundary.

### Section 6: Build a Life Larger Than Anxiety

**Section capability:** Choose values-linked action, respond constructively to
setbacks, maintain learning, and seek appropriate support.

#### Unit 6.1: Values and Direction · 7 nodes

1. **The Cost of Waiting for Calm** — Notice what symptom control has postponed.
2. **Value and Goal Are Different** — Separate ongoing direction from a finish line.
3. **Choose a Life Area** — Identify a meaningful direction without forced disclosure.
4. **Name the Direction Clearly** — Turn a vague ideal into a usable compass.
5. **Choose One Small Action** — Make the direction concrete and achievable.
6. **Act With Anxiety Present** — Preserve agency without requiring confidence.
7. **Values Checkpoint** — Connect approach practice with a chosen direction.

#### Unit 6.2: Setbacks and Self-Support · 8 nodes

1. **Review a Hard Attempt Accurately** — Separate difficulty from failure.
2. **What Was Learned?** — Find information even when the plan did not work.
3. **Notice Self-Blame** — Identify shame as another avoidance trigger.
4. **Setback or Changed Need?** — Distinguish normal fluctuation from worsening impairment.
5. **Restore Support** — Add structure after repeated difficulty.
6. **Re-Enter at the Right Step** — Reduce task difficulty without losing direction.
7. **Ask for Human Support** — Choose appropriate personal or professional help.
8. **Setback Checkpoint** — Build a recovery response for a changed situation.

#### Unit 6.3: Maintenance and Care Plan · 8 nodes

1. **Recognize Early Signs** — Notice worry, checking, or avoidance expanding again.
2. **Build the Pattern Map** — Connect personal cues, meanings, actions, and consequences.
3. **Build the Tool Map** — Match each maintaining process to a response.
4. **Plan Later Practice** — Keep important skills available through spaced retrieval.
5. **Shape the Environment** — Identify practical support for continued action.
6. **Set a Professional-Support Threshold** — Decide when self-guided practice is not enough.
7. **Set an Urgent-Care Threshold** — Keep crisis and medical routes clear and local.
8. **Course Checkpoint: From Alarm to Action** — Apply the full model to an unfamiliar case.

## 5. Retrieval and progression at high level

- The alarm and maintaining-cycle models return in every section using changed examples.
- Attention, worry, certainty seeking, and avoidance are taught separately before interleaving.
- Important skills return after a short gap, a unit gap, a section gap, and final transfer.
- Section 4 receives the most nodes because behavioural learning needs repeated attempts.
- Unit checkpoints test one integrated capability. Section checkpoints test changed-case transfer.
- Support reduces only after repeated evidence and returns after repeated errors.
- Personal reflection, symptom ratings, completion time, and mood change never count as mastery.

## 6. High-level safety and product rules

- No promise to remove, cure, reset, or control all anxiety.
- Grounding, breathing, or relaxation must not become proof that a situation is safe.
- Do not force trauma, feared-situation, health, or personal-value disclosures.
- Do not score feelings, symptoms, plans, or journaling as correct.
- Every activating lesson offers pause, skip, and return without penalty.
- Course progress remains after missed days.
- Severe impairment, self-harm risk, self-neglect, persistent worsening, or concerning
  physical symptoms surface professional or urgent-care guidance.
- The course never advises starting, changing, or stopping medication.
- Examples must work across cultures and avoid assumptions about family, work,
  relationships, or access to care.

## 7. Approval gate before mid level

High-level approval should settle:

1. the transdiagnostic scope;
2. the six-section learning sequence;
3. the 21-unit and 169-node curriculum size;
4. the heavier depth assigned to worry and behavioural approach;
5. the role and safety boundary of gradual approach practice;
6. whether panic and social anxiety stay as recognition units or later become
   separate specialist courses.

After approval, the mid level will define each lesson's objective, concepts,
prerequisites, misconceptions, mastery evidence, review links, difficulty, and
estimated duration. The low level will define exercise payloads, feedback,
hints, changed-example retries, accessibility states, and server data.
