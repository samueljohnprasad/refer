# Independent academic verification

**Scope.** Independent live scholarly-source spot-check of the seven load-bearing conclusions in `2026-10-30-academic-evidence.md`. Sources were opened on 2026-10-30. This is not a new systematic review, and project context was not used as evidence.

## Verdicts

### 1. Worked examples help novices — **VERIFIED**

A 2023 meta-analysis of worked examples in mathematics screened 8,033 abstracts and retained 55 studies with 181 effects. Worked examples produced a medium average benefit (*g* = 0.48), and correct examples were more beneficial than incorrect or mixed examples. The literature frames this advantage most strongly for initial skill acquisition and learners with little prior knowledge.

- Direct source: [Barbieri et al., *A Meta-analysis of the Worked Examples Effect on Mathematics Performance*](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1)
- Accessible author-hosted paper: [full paper PDF](https://www.danamillercotto.com/uploads/4/7/7/2/47725475/barbieri_et_al__2023__we_meta-analysis.pdf)

**Transfer limit:** This evidence is primarily mathematics instruction, not CBT, sensitive self-disclosure, app onboarding, or clinical outcomes. It supports using instructional scaffolding for unfamiliar tasks; it does not determine the ideal number, length, or emotional content of onboarding examples.

### 2. Self-reference supports memory, while benefits of digital mental-health personalization remain inconclusive — **VERIFIED**

The classic self-reference meta-analysis found superior memory after self-referential encoding compared with semantic and other-referential encoding. Separately, a 2023 systematic review of 94 adult depression-focused digital mental-health interventions found personalization in 66% of interventions, but only two small trials directly manipulated personalization; the review explicitly judged evidence of benefit scarce and inconclusive.

- Direct source: [Symons & Johnson, *The self-reference effect in memory: a meta-analysis*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9136641/)
- Direct source: [Hornstein et al., *Personalization strategies in digital mental health interventions*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2023.1170002/full)

**Transfer limit:** The self-reference effect is a memory-encoding result, not evidence that asking for personal mental-health material is safer, more acceptable, or more effective on first use. The digital review is restricted to interventions for adult depressive symptoms and notes search and terminology limitations. “Self-relevant” and “personalized intervention” are related but not interchangeable constructs.

### 3. Active generation and self-explanation generally help learning — **VERIFIED**

A self-explanation meta-analysis reported 69 eligible effects from 64 reports and a pooled effect of *g* = 0.55. A separate generation-effect meta-analysis summarized 445 effects across 86 studies and found a mean advantage of about 0.40 for generating rather than reading material. At classroom scale, a 225-study undergraduate STEM meta-analysis found active learning improved exam/concept-inventory performance by 0.47 SD and reduced failure relative to lecture.

- Direct source: [Bisra et al., *Inducing Self-Explanation: A Meta-Analysis*](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1186664)
- Direct source: [Bertsch et al., *The generation effect: a meta-analytic review*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645161/)
- Direct source: [Freeman et al., *Active learning increases student performance in science, engineering, and mathematics*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24821756/)

**Transfer limit:** These are educational and memory outcomes, not clinical or onboarding outcomes. “Active learning,” “generation,” and “self-explanation” are not identical interventions. Prompt quality, time on task, prior knowledge, and cognitive load matter; notably, the worked-example meta-analysis found that adding self-explanation prompts did not automatically improve the worked-example package.

### 4. Autonomy-supportive interventions show small health-behavior effects — **VERIFIED**

A meta-analysis of 65 independent randomized tests found a small average effect of self-determination-theory interventions on health behavior (*d* = 0.23). Publication and small-study bias were present, but corrected estimates remained positive at approximately *d* = 0.15 or above. Meta-analytic structural equation modeling supported mediation through autonomous motivation and perceived competence. A later synthesis found that interventions which actually increased autonomous motivation produced larger behavior change than interventions which did not.

- Direct source: [Sheeran et al., *Self-determination theory interventions for health behavior change*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32437175/)
- Direct source: [Sheeran et al., *Does increasing autonomous motivation or perceived competence lead to health behavior change?*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34881939/)

**Transfer limit:** These are heterogeneous health behaviors such as activity, diet, alcohol use, smoking cessation, and sedentary behavior. They do not directly test a choice screen, skip affordance, notification request, or forced commitment in mental-health onboarding. The evidence supports autonomy as a general design direction, not any exact UI treatment.

### 5. Mental-health gamification is not established to improve efficacy or adherence — **VERIFIED**

In a meta-analysis of 38 studies and 8,110 participants, the number of gamification elements did not significantly predict depressive-symptom outcomes or adherence. A 2025 synthesis of 92 mental-health-app RCTs likewise found no significant association between the number or domain of persuasive-design principles and efficacy or program completion, while emphasizing inconsistent engagement measurement. These are null moderator results, so the defensible conclusion is “not established,” not “proven ineffective.”

- Direct source: [Six et al., *Examining the Effectiveness of Gamification in Mental Health Apps for Depression*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8669581/)
- Direct source: [McCall et al., *A meta-analysis of persuasive design, engagement, and efficacy in 92 RCTs of mental health apps*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-01567-5)

**Transfer limit:** Both analyses code packages or counts of features rather than randomizing an isolated reward, streak, or repair mechanic. The depression-focused analysis does not establish effects for anxiety or other populations, and null meta-regression cannot prove equivalence. Persuasive design is broader than gamification.

### 6. Implementation intentions have stronger general evidence than forced pledges — **VERIFIED, WITH NARROWING**

The broad implementation-intention evidence is real. A 2025 Annual Review synthesis reports a weighted mean of *d* = 0.46 across 12 meta-analyses, while a 642-test meta-analysis reports an unadjusted average of *d* = 0.36, approximately *d* = 0.35 after trim-and-fill, and a smaller robust-Bayesian estimate of *d* = 0.15 in the presence of publication bias and heterogeneity. The review explicitly distinguishes contingent if-then planning from mere goal commitment and reports better results for contingent plans than noncontingent schedules.

- Direct source: [Gollwitzer & Sheeran, *Psychology of Planning*](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-021524-110536)
- Direct source: [Sheeran, Listrom, & Gollwitzer, *The when and how of planning*](https://doi.org/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563)

**Transfer limit:** The literature supports if-then plans over mere goal intentions or noncontingent scheduling in general. It does **not** directly test a voluntary if-then plan against a coercive digital mental-health onboarding “pledge.” That exact product comparison remains **UNVERIFIED**. Effects are heterogeneous, behavior effects are smaller than some cognitive or affective effects, and plan benefit depends on an already valued goal, cue-response quality, and commitment.

### 7. Brief reappraisal can shift proximal interpretation or emotion without broader symptom change — **VERIFIED**

In a small experiment with 35 healthy adults using personally salient negative self-beliefs, reappraisal reduced immediate negative emotion compared with reacting, though it required greater cognitive-control resources. In a 2026 randomized trial with 162 college students, a digital single-session reappraisal intervention improved online and offline interpretation bias at post-test and two-week follow-up but was largely ineffective for anxiety and depressive symptoms. Together these directly support a proximal-change / broader-outcome boundary.

- Direct source: [Goldin, Moodie, & Gross, *Acceptance versus reappraisal: Behavioral, autonomic, and neural effects*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30656602/)
- Direct source: [Ladis & Teachman, *Development and Evaluation of a Digital Single-Session Reappraisal Intervention*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13255085/)

**Transfer limit:** The lab study was small, involved healthy adults, and measured immediate emotion and physiology rather than symptoms or retention. The digital trial involved college students selected for negative social-media self-evaluations and tested interpretation bias, not general CBT onboarding. Neither study establishes that a proximal shift is an onboarding “aha,” improves adherence, or produces clinical benefit.

## Overall assessment

No load-bearing conclusion was overturned. Six are verified as carefully bounded generalizations. The implementation-intention conclusion is verified only when “forced pledge” is narrowed to mere goal commitment or noncontingent planning; superiority over a coercive onboarding pledge UI remains unverified. Across all seven conclusions, the proposed product sequence remains a design inference because no source tests the complete first-use mental-health onboarding flow end to end.
