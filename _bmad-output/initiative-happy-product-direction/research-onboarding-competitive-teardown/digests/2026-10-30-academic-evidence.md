# Academic evidence digest: first-use CBT and digital mental-health onboarding

**Research question.** What evidence supports self-relevant practice rather than canned demonstrations, active learning rather than passive explanation, autonomy rather than forced commitment, rewards and streaks, implementation intentions and reminders, and cognitive reappraisal as an early “aha” moment?

**Access date for every source:** 2026-10-30.

## Bottom line

The evidence best supports a **scaffolded hybrid**, not an all-or-nothing choice: show one short, clear worked example, then invite the person to apply the same move to a personally relevant but manageable situation. Self-reference improves memory, active generation usually improves learning, and autonomy-supportive interventions can improve health behaviour; however, direct evidence that this exact sequence improves first-use digital mental-health onboarding is sparse. Personalization research in digital mental health remains inconclusive [S1–S8].

Rewards, streaks, and reminders are not established therapeutic ingredients. They may alter engagement, but effects are mixed and often small. A streak can motivate while intact and discourage action when broken; tangible or controlling rewards can also crowd out intrinsic motivation. Use progress feedback and forgiving continuity, not loss-framed pressure [S9–S15].

An opt-in **if-then plan** (“If it is after lunch, then I will do one two-minute practice”) has a much stronger general behaviour-change evidence base than a forced pledge. Notifications can increase immediate opens or actions, but fixed reminders have not reliably improved sustained engagement [S14–S17].

Cognitive reappraisal can produce an immediate, perceptible shift in negative emotion or interpretation, including in personally salient material. That makes a tiny before/after reappraisal exercise a plausible onboarding “aha.” But “aha moment” itself has not been isolated as a causal onboarding mechanism, and brief digital reappraisal has often changed the proximal skill or interpretation without changing broader symptom outcomes [S18–S21]. Treat it as a **skill demonstration**, not proof of treatment.

## Methods

- Live web searches were run on 2026-10-30 across PubMed/PMC, journal and publisher pages, ERIC, Annual Reviews, PNAS, Frontiers, JMIR, and Springer-hosted materials.
- Priority was: peer-reviewed meta-analyses and systematic reviews; randomized or microrandomized trials; then foundational experimental work. Recent sources were preferred, with older foundational evidence retained where it directly addressed self-reference, rewards, or active learning.
- Every conclusion below is based on a source retrieved in this run. Project files, product claims, and ambient context were not used as evidence.
- Evidence labels:
  - **Lab/learning:** memory, instructional, or emotion-regulation experiments outside a clinical onboarding setting.
  - **Clinical efficacy:** mental-health intervention outcome evidence; not assumed to transfer to engagement or onboarding.
  - **Engagement:** app use, adherence, completion, or proximal action; not assumed to imply wellbeing benefit.
  - **Product-design inference:** a bounded translation from research to onboarding, explicitly not a directly tested finding.
- This is a decision digest, not a clinical guideline or systematic review with exhaustive database screening. No diagnosis or treatment claim is made.

## Theme synthesis

### 1. Self-relevant practice should follow, not necessarily replace, a worked example

Relating information to oneself supports encoding and recall [S1]. That is relevant to first-use practice because a person-generated situation supplies organization, prior knowledge, and emotional meaning that a generic scenario lacks. Yet the strongest digital mental-health personalization review found prevalence, not decisive benefit: personalization was common, but comparative evidence was scarce and inconsistent [S2].

Meanwhile, worked examples are beneficial for novices in other learning domains [S3]. The safe inference is therefore a sequence:

1. Show one small, correct example that makes the task legible.
2. Ask the person to try the same structure with a recent, low-stakes situation.
3. Preserve “use another example” and “skip” paths.

This avoids two unsupported extremes: a long canned walkthrough with no application, and an immediate demand for personal disclosure before trust or task understanding exists.

### 2. Active learning is stronger than explanation alone, but the first action must be light

Meta-analytic learning evidence supports active generation and self-explanation [S4, S5]. It also warns that active learning can feel less effective because effort is misread as failure [S6]. For onboarding, the first action should therefore be small enough to complete in roughly one or two minutes, scaffolded, and followed by an immediate explanation of what just happened.

A full blank thought record is likely too demanding as a first interaction. A better evidence-aligned form is constrained generation: choose a recent situation, select or name the first interpretation, then generate or select one credible alternative. This preserves active processing without making the user invent an entire therapeutic formulation.

### 3. Autonomy support is better grounded than forced commitment

Autonomy-supportive interventions show small but consistent health-behaviour effects, with evidence that autonomous motivation and perceived competence are part of the mechanism [S7, S8]. This is not direct proof that a skip button or topic picker increases mental-health app retention, but it argues against coercive first-use patterns.

Useful autonomy is bounded. Offer two or three meaningful choices, not a large catalogue. A user can choose:

- “Something that happened today” or “show me an example”;
- which of a few broad situations feels relevant;
- whether to create a plan;
- whether, when, and how to receive a reminder.

Do not require a streak pledge, notification permission, calendar schedule, or ambitious goal before the person experiences the core skill.

### 4. Rewards can acknowledge mastery; streaks should never punish interruption

The direct mental-health app evidence does not show that adding persuasive-design elements, gamification, or rewards reliably improves either outcomes or adherence [S9, S10]. Some package-level pediatric findings are positive, but they do not isolate the game mechanic and do not generalize cleanly to adult first-use CBT [S11].

Streak research shows a specific hazard: an intact streak can become its own goal, and a visible break can reduce subsequent motivation; repair attenuates the damage [S12]. Older reward research further suggests expected tangible rewards can undermine voluntary engagement with already interesting activities [S13].

Design implication: use immediate competence feedback (“You found another possible reading”), visible cumulative practice, and celebratory motion. If continuity is shown, make it forgiving: count “practices this week,” allow recovery, and never reset the user’s identity or progress to zero after one missed day.

### 5. Ask for an if-then plan after value; make reminders optional and specific

Implementation intentions have a broad replicated evidence base [S14]. Their mechanism depends on linking a concrete cue to a concrete response, not on making a vague promise. A good onboarding close is therefore:

> If **[chosen cue]** happens, then I will **[tiny action]**.

Examples include “If I close my laptop after lunch, then I will do one two-minute thought check” or “If I notice I am replaying a conversation, then I will write one alternative interpretation.” The user should create or select both parts.

Reminders are a separate decision. They can improve near-term engagement [S16, S17], but systematic evidence is mixed and fixed policies have not consistently improved sustained use [S15, S17]. Ask after the plan is meaningful; default to the user’s chosen cue; make declining easy; and avoid guilt-based copy.

### 6. Reappraisal can be the first perceptible payoff, not a promise of symptom change

Reappraisal is well suited to a first-use demonstration because it can create a visible contrast: event unchanged, interpretation changed, emotional intensity sometimes shifts [S18]. Recent digital trials show the important boundary. A brief program can change interpretation bias or preserve reported skill use without outperforming a credible active control on broad symptom outcomes [S19, S20]. Other single-session programs have produced null results [S21].

The evidence-aligned “aha” is therefore modest:

- capture an initial interpretation and 0–10 believability or intensity rating;
- help generate one plausible, balanced alternative—not forced positivity;
- rerate;
- reflect the difference without implying diagnosis, recovery, or treatment.

If there is no rating change, that is not failure. The skill demonstrated is cognitive flexibility: more than one interpretation can coexist. The direct claim that this moment improves onboarding retention remains untested.

## Contrary and null findings

- **Personalization:** common in digital mental health, but its incremental benefit remains empirically scarce and inconclusive [S2]. Self-reference memory findings cannot prove that immediate personal disclosure is preferable in a sensitive app [S1].
- **Worked examples:** demonstrations can outperform unsupported problem solving for novices [S3]. “Active” should not mean removing scaffolding.
- **Self-explanation:** the overall effect is positive [S4], but the worked-example meta-analysis found smaller effects in studies that added self-explanation prompts than in worked examples without them [S3]. Prompt burden, quality, and cross-study confounding matter.
- **Autonomy:** self-determination interventions had only small average effects and showed publication/small-study bias [S7]. Too much choice can add cognitive burden; this digest found no direct first-session CBT onboarding trial comparing free choice with a forced commitment.
- **Persuasive design and gamification:** a 2025 meta-analysis found no significant association between number/domain of persuasive principles and efficacy or completion [S9]. A depression-app meta-analysis found no adherence or symptom advantage from gamification [S10].
- **Streaks:** the strongest break/repair evidence is consumer-behaviour research, not digital mental health [S12]. It supports caution, not a claim that all streaks are harmful.
- **Reminders:** technology-supported engagement strategies in digital mental health have mixed findings [S15]. Immediate opens do not establish sustained engagement or benefit [S16, S17].
- **Reappraisal:** it can require cognitive effort [S18]. Brief digital trials have reported skill-specific gains with no broader outcome advantage [S19, S20] and fully null comparisons [S21].

## Transfer limits

1. **No direct end-to-end onboarding trial.** The retrieved literature does not test the complete proposed first-use sequence against alternatives in a representative app population.
2. **Education is not therapy.** Active learning, self-explanation, and worked-example studies mainly involve academic tasks. They inform instructional mechanics, not clinical efficacy.
3. **Engagement is not benefit.** Opens, completion, time in app, and streak continuation are product behaviours. They must not be described as mental-health improvement.
4. **Clinical outcome evidence is not feature attribution.** Package-level trials cannot show that a reward, reminder, or reappraisal screen caused the outcome.
5. **Populations differ.** Evidence spans students, community adults, office workers, and youth; it may not transfer across age, culture, distress level, accessibility needs, or clinical context.
6. **Sensitive self-reference can add burden.** The self-reference effect is a memory phenomenon. Asking for a personally painful example before trust, consent, and safety are established may be counterproductive; this was not directly tested.
7. **“Aha” is a product construct.** Reappraisal studies test emotion, interpretation, skill use, or symptoms—not whether a delightful insight drives retention.

## Design implications, ranked by evidence fit

### Strongest defensible moves

1. **Use a worked-example-to-personal-practice bridge.** One concise example, then one optional self-relevant attempt [S1–S5].
2. **Make the user do one meaningful cognitive move.** Select or generate a plausible alternative interpretation; do not front-load explanation [S4–S6].
3. **Offer bounded autonomy.** Let the user choose example vs personal situation, topic, plan, and reminder; never gate first value on commitment [S7, S8].
4. **End with an optional implementation intention.** Link a user-chosen cue to a tiny practice [S14].
5. **Ask for reminder consent after the plan.** Allow timing/channel control and easy refusal [S15–S17].

### Moves to use cautiously

6. **Celebrate demonstrated skill, not emotional outcome.** Feedback can acknowledge effort and competence, but rewards are not established therapeutic ingredients [S9–S13].
7. **Prefer forgiving continuity to a daily streak.** Weekly counts, grace, and repair avoid turning one missed day into a visible total loss [S12].
8. **Frame reappraisal as flexibility, not positivity.** “Another possible reading” is safer and more accurate than insisting the first thought is wrong [S18–S21].
9. **Use a before/after rating only as immediate feedback.** No change is acceptable; do not imply a clinical result [S18–S21].

### What should be tested in-product

- Worked example only vs worked example followed by optional self-relevant practice.
- Personal practice first vs example-first, with distress, completion, perceived safety, and skill comprehension measured separately.
- Optional plan vs no plan; plan quality should record cue specificity and action size.
- No continuity display vs forgiving weekly progress vs repairable streak.
- Reminder opt-in timing and user-chosen timing; assess 7- and 28-day practice, notification disablement, and perceived pressure.
- Whether the reappraisal interaction changes proximal understanding and return behaviour; do not use symptom change as a first-session success proxy.

## Open evidence gaps

- No retrieved trial isolates self-relevant CBT practice versus a matched canned demonstration during first use.
- No retrieved study establishes a forced commitment as superior to an optional plan in first-use digital mental health.
- No mental-health onboarding trial retrieved here isolates streak repair, grace days, or weekly accumulation.
- No retrieved study tests whether a reappraisal “aha” causes later retention, practice transfer, or wellbeing benefit.
- A direct factorial onboarding experiment is needed before any of these product mechanisms can be described as proven for this context.
